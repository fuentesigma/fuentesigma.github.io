On Frames Of Reference

December 2024

In this brief essay, I review the role of reference frames in physics, in particular, their influence on physical observables such as energy, entropy, and quantum spectra. Beginning within a classical Lagrangian formalism, I revisit how gravitational potentials emerge as pseudo-forces in non-inertial frames, differing from the Newtonian geometrisation. Entropy is another quantity that depends on the frame of reference, which immediately leads to observe how gravitational potentials modify local thermodynamic quantities. Going further, the quantised energy spectrum of a free-falling electron in non-inertial frames outlines implications for spinor fields in curved spacetime via the Dirac equation. As well, it is discussed how gauge transformations and reference frame shifts occasionally produce similar physical effects despite being geometrically different.

Gravity Is Not A Force

Let \(m\) be the mass of a free-falling classical object and \(\mathbf{x}\) its position vector wrt. an inertial frame of reference. Suppose there are no external forces acting on the particle, and its internal degrees of freedom as well as its charge, if any, are irrelevant to its motion. The Lagrangian for this particle is given by:

eq. 1 \[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{\mathbf{x}}^2. \]

One can shift to a non-inertial frame of reference accelerating upward at constant acceleration \(\mathbf{g}.\) In this new coordinate system, the particle is described by the position vector \(\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{g}t^2,\) where \(t\) determines the time at which the particle has been displaced a length \(|\mathbf{x}_0|\) from the origin. An expression for the non-inertial Lagrangian is obtained by differentiating \(\mathbf{x}'\) wrt. time and substituting it into eq. 1:

eq. 2 \[ \mathcal{L}' = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{'2} - m \mathbf{g} \cdot \mathbf{x}', \]

where the term \(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t (m \mathbf{x}' \cdot \mathbf{g} t)\) has been neglected since a total time derivative finds no influence within the principle of least action.

The extra term \(- m \mathbf{g} \cdot \mathbf{x}'\) in eq. 2 acts like a potential produced solely by a shift of reference frame. In consequence, from the Euler-Lagrange equations of motion the effective force \(-\nabla(-m \mathbf{g} \cdot \mathbf{x}' ) = m \mathbf{g}\) is interpreted as the gravitational force in the accelerated frame, although not owing its presence to any interaction. Gravity, thereby, appears as a pseudo-force being at odds with Newton’s derivation, in that the latter posits a law of universal gravitation proportional to the product of two masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation. Which one is correct?

I shall put things this way. The escape velocity is the minimum speed a massive particle must attain to break free from a gravitational field without any further propulsion. For a spherically symmetric mass \(M\) with radius \(r\), the escape velocity for a particle of mass \(m\) over such a body's surface is derived from energy conservation as:

eq. 3 \[ \frac{1}{2}mv_\text{esc}^2 = \frac{GMm}{r} \qquad \implies \qquad v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}, \]

and \(G\) is the gravitational constant.

In a free-falling frame, the particle follows a geodesic and feels no gravitational force; hence, escape velocity loses meaning there. By contrast, in a stationary frame, gravity enters as a potential in eq. 2, behaves force-wise, and the concept of escape velocity remains valid. Newton’s law quantified this effect but never showed how masses communicate their mutual pull. It was Einstein who later clarified that gravity emerges from spacetime curvature rather than from a literal force by showing that spacetime geometry is proportional to matter and energy.

One of the simplest exact solutions to the Einstein's field equations was found by Schwarzschild, who imposed spherical symmetry on the spacetime. The resulting line element is found to be:

eq. 4 \[ \mathrm{d}s^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r c^2}\right)c^2 \mathrm{d}t^2 + \left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r c^2}\right)^{-1} \mathrm{d}r^2 + r^2 \mathrm{d}\Omega^2. \]

Here \(\mathrm{d}\Omega^2\) represents the metric on the 2-sphere and inherits the \(SO(3)\) rotational symmetry of the problem . As seen, at \(r = r_s = 2GM/c^2\), the temporal factor vanishes while the radial term diverges, signalling a coordinate singularity known as the Schwarzschild radius. This radius defines the horizon of a black hole, beyond which the radial coordinate fails to remain spacelike, and light itself cannot break free as the escape velocity \(v_\text{esc}\) becomes \(c\). In other words, the inward pull is so severe that every trajectory, even an outward-oriented geodesic, eventually curves back. Thus the event horizon at \(r = r_s\) dictates no path leads outwards. The notion of escape velocity, once so practical in Newtonian contexts, now reflects a fundamental speed limit built into spacetime itself: it is not about overpowering a force but about whether there exists any causal path to infinity.

Topologically, each 2-sphere for fixed \(r\) closes smoothly, reflecting how all directions about the central mass are equivalent. The full set of isometries for Schwarzschild spacetime includes a time-translation symmetry and these spatial rotations, collectively forming a group \(SO(3)\times\mathbb{R}\).

To reconcile the fact that gravity manifests as a pseudo-force in a non-inertial frame—as eq. 2—yet yields a tangible escape velocity. In this frame the geometry of spacetime encodes a real condition on the energy required to depart the mass distribution—further details in next section. Hence the escape velocity emerges from the underlying curvature of spacetime: it does not hinge on an actual interaction but on whether a geodesic can extend to infinity. In this way, an apparent pseudo-force in one frame becomes a very real energy threshold in another, even if gravity itself arises from a shift in reference frame, whether for massive particles or not.

Speaking of which, photons cannot be assigned an inertial frame of reference because inertial coordinates require massive observers whose world-lines satisfy \(\mathrm{d}s^2 < 0\). For light, \(\mathrm{d}s^2 = 0\), so its trajectory is null rather than timelike. In flat spacetime with the Minkowski line element \(\mathrm{d}s^2 = -c^2 \, \mathrm{d}t^2 + \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^2\), the so-called light-cone geometry enforces that any path taken by a photon satisfies \(|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}| = \pm c \, \mathrm{d}t\). There is no valid inertial rest frame in which the photon sees itself outside time, and thus the erroneous claim that photons travel instantaneously from their own viewpoint lacks meaning. Conceptualising a coordinate system co-moving with light is simply inconsistent with the requirement that \(\mathrm{d}s^2 < 0\) for inertial observers. Hence, from our frame, photons take time to traverse space, and from theirs, the notion of travelling does not even exist!

Extending this notion to curved spacetime—such as the Schwarzschild solution eq. 4—the same principle holds. A photon follows a null geodesic irrespective of coordinate choices, and the apparent force of gravity emerges from the geometry rather than from any frame-dependent potential. Once \(r\) reaches the Schwarzschild radius, light continues moving locally at speed \(c\) along null geodesics. No rest frame can attach to a photon there or anywhere else, reaffirming that frames of reference and inertial coordinates remain strictly a timelike privilege. The geometry—flat or curved—simply does not permit a viewpoint wherein photons instantaneously arrive at their destination.

Entropy Is Frame-dependent

The distinctions do not terminate here. Entropy and energy are observable quantities that depend on the chosen frame of reference. To illustrate these facts, we can analyse the thermodynamics of eq. 1 and eq. 2 and discus any discernible differences. To commence, a straightforward Legendre transformation of eq. 1 yields the following Hamiltonian:

eq. 5 \[ H = \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m}, \]

where \(\mathbf{p}\) is the canonical momentum in the inertial frame. Likewise, in the context of eq. 2 the corresponding Hamiltonian becomes:

eq. 6 \[ H' = \frac{\mathbf{p}^{'2}}{2m} + m\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{x'}. \]

Because both Hamiltonians do not show explicit time dependence, they represent the energy of the particle in each of the frames of reference. Regarding the inertial frame, the phase space volume will depend only on the momentum, and so the density of states:

eq. 7 \[ \Omega \propto \int \mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} \, \delta\left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m} - E\right), \]

consequently, the microcanonical entropy \(S = k_B \ln \Omega\) will solely depend on the total energy \(E\), which is exclusively kinetic in the inertial frame of reference and kinetic plus potential energy in the case of the non-inertial frame.

If instead of a microcanonical ensemble, it is a canonical ensemble how the thermodynamic universe looks like, then energy is exchanged with a heat bath at a fixed temperature \(T\). In this case, the partition function in the inertial frame of reference can be obtained provided the Hamiltonian eq. 5:

eq. 8 \[ Z = \int_\mathbb{R} \mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} \, \exp\left(-\beta H\right), \]

the Lagrange factor \(\beta\) is identified with the inverse energy \(1/k_B T\). The integral over spatial coordinates determines the volume \(V\) of the ensemble, while the sum over momenta is a standard Gaussian integral. The partition function is then:

eq. 9 \[ Z = C V \left( \frac{2\pi m }{\beta} \right)^{3/2}, \]

where \(C\) is a constant that comes from normalisation.

In a canonical ensemble the entropy is equal to \(k_B (\ln Z + \beta E)\), where \(E\) is the average energy totalling \(3k_B T/2\). Evidently, the entropy in the inertial frame is:

eq. 10 \[ S = k_B \left[ \ln C + \ln V + \frac{3}{2}\left(1 + \ln 2\pi m k_B T \right)\right]. \]

On the other hand, from a non-inertial observer, the free-falling particle attractively moves along the direction of the unit normal to the surface of the non-inertial frame of reference. Without any loss of generality, assume the particle moves along the \(Oz'\) axis, thus the partition function at thermal equilibrium is to be computed as:

eq. 11 \[ Z' = \int_\mathbb{R} \mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}'\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}' \, \exp -\beta \left(\frac{p^{'2}}{2m} + mgz'\right). \]

The sum over momenta has been previously obtained. The remaining calculation involves the sum over spatial coordinates, \(\int_{\mathbb{R}} \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}’ \, \exp(-\beta mgz’)\). Assuming significant height differences, the integral over the axis of motion determines the average height \(\langle z \rangle = 1/\beta m g\), while the integral over the transverse coordinates defines the area \(A_\perp\), which is perpendicular to the gravitational direction. Therefore, the partition function in the non-inertial frame reads:

eq. 12 \[ Z' = C' \frac{A_\perp}{\beta m g} \left( \frac{2\pi m }{\beta} \right)^{3/2}, \]

\(C'\) stands for a normalisation constant.

The average energy in the non-inertial frame takes into account the potential term in eq. 6. A simple calculation shows that \(E' = 3k_BT/2 + mg\langle z \rangle\), and so the expression for entropy takes the form:

eq. 13 \[ S' = k_B \left[ \ln C' + \ln A_\perp + \frac{3}{2} \ln 2\pi m k_B T -\ln\left( \frac{m g}{k_B T} \right) + \frac{5}{2} \right], \]

hence, the volume integral returns dependence on temperature, not only because there is exchange with a heat bath in the canonical ensemble, but because the gravitational potential modifies the density of accessible microstates. The temperature thereby does not directly correspond to some microscopic interaction; instead, \(T\) represents the temperature required to statistically balance the gravitational potential energy with thermal fluctuations. This is why the average height \(\langle z \rangle\) in the non-inertial frame is influenced by temperature—I shall discuss this in the next section with more detail.

Furthermore, subtracting eq. 10 from eq. 13 reveals that the entropy difference between the two frames is only determined by the volumes of motion in each frame (up to normalisation constants).

eq. 14 \[ \Delta S \propto \ln \left( \frac{k_B T A_\perp}{mg} \right) - \log V + 1, \]

interestingly, the volume in the non-inertial frame depends on a thermal bath at fixed temperature \(T\). Why is energy different in the inertial frame compared to the energy in the non-inertial frame? The answer is simple: there is no free lunch. It is not the same thing that moves in one frame compared to another. In the inertial frame, the kinetic energy of the free-falling object is the relevant subject, while in the non-inertial frame, it is the kinetic energy of the free-falling object plus the energy of the accelerated observer. Energy is indeed conserved in both frames, and the first law of thermodynamics holds true for both. Ultimately, one can effortlessly determine the actual energy value in the inertial frame of reference as perceived from the non-inertial frame by employing a Lorentz transformation, and conversely.

Extending these ideas to General Relativity and black holes involves recognising that gravity is encoded in spacetime curvature rather than in a mere potential. Although a uniform gravitational field can be mimicked by an accelerating observer in flat spacetime, real astrophysical objects (e.g. black holes) impose curved geometry modelled by solutions of Einstein’s field equations such as the Schwarzschild metric eq. 4. In that setting, one cannot define a global inertial frame that encompasses the entire spacetime, particularly near horizons. Nonetheless, local observers still perceive themselves as inertial if they undergo free fall, recovering flat Minkowski geometry in their immediate neighbourhood. Entropy must then be revisited in this curved context: if we try to construct something like a global partition function, the very notion of energy (and thus thermodynamic variables) depends on identifying a timelike Killing vector for the entire spacetime. For Schwarzschild black holes, the relevant Killing vector exists only outside the event horizon, so a global comparison with an accelerated observer is subtler than in flat space.

Despite these complications, a striking example of gravity–thermodynamics interplay emerges in the form of black hole thermodynamics. For a Schwarzschild black hole of mass \(M\), one finds that the horizon at \(r_s\) behaves like a thermal surface with Hawking temperature \(T_\text{H} = \hbar\,c^3/(8\pi G M\,k_B)\). Likewise, Bekenstein and Hawking showed that the entropy of a black hole is

eq. 15 \[ S_{\text{BH}} = k_B \,\frac{A}{4\,\ell_{\mathrm{p}}^2}, \]

where \(A\) is the horizon area and \(\ell_{\mathrm{p}}^2 = \hbar G/c^3\) sets the fundamental Planck scale—the constant \(\hbar\) has the dimensions of an action, mostly associated across physics literature with the reduced Planck constant. These results highlight the fact that energy and entropy become horizon-sensitive and cannot be treated as purely local constructs. Just as the free-falling observer in flat space sees no gravitational potential, an observer dropping into a black hole does not feel a force at the horizon. Yet an observer hovering at a fixed radius perceives a tremendous gravitational effect that shapes the very definitions of temperature and entropy. The moral remains: different frames—particularly in curved spacetimes—alter the ways we formulate energy, partition functions, and thermodynamic behaviour, with black hole horizons providing the most dramatic realisation of how reference frames and geometry intertwine.

Energy Spectra Are Frame-Dependent

What if we extend the analysis to the motion of a free electron in inertial and non-inertial frames of reference? Although the earlier discussion on entropy and energy provided some hint into the potential conclusion of this story, we shall witness its unfolding in its entirety.

To start with, the symmetrisation of the Hamiltonian eq. 5 leads to the quantum Hamiltonian:

eq. 16 \[ \hat{H} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m_e}, \]

where \(m_e\) is the mass of the electron and \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}\) the momentum operator acting in a space whose details are of no relevance for the moment. Since the electron is freely moving, gravity disappears and the Schrödinger equation reduces to a time-independent eigenvalue problem:

eq. 17 \[ \hat{H}\psi = E \psi, \]

here \(\psi\) is the wave function (non-square integrable) and \(E\) is an eigenvalue of \(\hat{H}\). According to Sturm–Liouville theory, the wave function is given by plane eigenwaves \(\psi(\mathbf{x}) = \exp(\mathrm{i} \mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})\), where \(\mathbf{k}\) is the wave vector. This means:

eq. 18 \[ \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m_e}\psi(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{(-\mathrm{i}\hbar\nabla)^2}{2m_e} \exp(\mathrm{i} \mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}), \]

and, consequently, the energy has a continuum spectrum given by \(E=\hbar^2k^2/2m_e\). This completes the solution.

On the other hand, the symmetrisation of eq. 6 leads to:

eq. 19 \[ \hat{H}' = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{'2}}{2m_e} + m_e\mathbf{g}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}', \]

where \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}'\) is the position operator and \(\mathbf{g}\) remains classical.

As before, I will assume the electron moves radially towards the surface of the spherical body causing the spacetime geometry be curved. This is a fair assumption without risk of reducing generality since no other forces are considered within this scenario, hence any electron's precession can be disregarded. Thus, substituting the momentum operator explicitly into the latest expression, and using \(\hat{z}\psi(\hat{z}) = z \psi(\hat{z})\), eq. 17 takes the form:

eq. 20 \[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2 m_e} \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 \psi}{\mathrm{d}z^2} + \left(m_e g z - E \right)\psi = 0. \]

this is the Schrödinger equation for a particle in a linear potential, which is mathematically identical to the equation governing the Airy functions \(\text{Ai}(x)\) and \(\text{Bi}(x)\), of the first and second kind, respectively. Therefore, the general solution to eq. 20 is expressed as:

\[ \psi(z) = C_1 \, \text{Ai}\left[\frac{z - \frac{E}{m_e g}}{\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2 m_e^2 g}\right)^{1/3}}\right] + C_2 \, \text{Bi}\left[\frac{z - \frac{E}{m_e g}}{\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2 m_e^2 g}\right)^{1/3}}\right], \]

\(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are normalisation constants.

Given the physically acceptable wavefunction must remain finite as \(z \to \infty\), then \(C_2 = 0\). Thus, the physically meaningful solution is:

eq. 21 \[ \psi(z) = C_1 \, \text{Ai}\left[\frac{z - \frac{E}{m_e g}}{\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2 m_e^2 g}\right)^{1/3}}\right]. \]

The quantisation of energy arises from the boundary conditions on the wavefunction. For large negative values of \(z\), the wavefunction must decay to zero. This constraint implies that the energy eigenvalues are determined by the zeroes of the Airy function \(\text{Ai}(x)\). Substituting the wavefunction into the Sturm-Liouville problem eq. 17, one finds the eigenenergies are given by:

eq. 22 \[ E_n = \left(\frac{m_e g^2 \hbar^2}{2}\right)^{1/3} a_n, \]

with \(a_n\) the \(n\)th zeroes of the Airy function \(\text{Ai}(x)\).

Therefore, the energy spectrum is quantised when the electron is seen falling in a non-inertial frame of reference, with eigenvalues determined by eq. 22. In this case, the wavefunctions are square integrable and decay exponentially for large negative \(z\). In brief, the accelerated observer introduces a ladder-like energy structure for the electron, with spacings determined by the gravitational potential energy scale. This generalises the previous discussion for classical, non-charged particles at thermal equilibrium.

Now, it is time to step up the game and treat the problem within the formalism of quantum field theory in curved spacetime. This analysis allows us to consider the behaviour of a quantum field under gravitational effects as well as how an electron field transforms under inertial and non-inertial frames. In addition, the role of spacetime curvature and the equivalence principle in the dynamics of spinor fields are manifest.

In flat Minkowski spacetime, it is the Dirac equation which governs the dynamics of relativistic spin-\(\frac{1}{2}\) particles:

eq. 23 \[ (i \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0, \]

where \(\gamma^\mu\) are the gamma matrices satisfying the Clifford algebra \(\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2 \eta^{\mu \nu}\), with \(\eta^{\mu \nu}\) being the Minkowski metric, and \(\psi\) is the Dirac spinor field, encoding the electron's degrees of freedom. This equation of motion describes electrons in inertial reference frames, where spacetime is flat and gravity is absent.

To see explicitly how the relativistic energies differ between an inertial frame and a uniformly accelerating (or weak-field gravitational) frame, we can proceed by analogy to the non-relativistic case. In an inertial frame with flat Minkowski spacetime, the free Dirac Hamiltonian for an electron reads:

eq. 24 \[ \hat{H}_0 = c \,\alpha\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}} + m_e c^2 \beta, \]

where \(m_e\) is the electron mass, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the Dirac matrices (satisfying the usual anti-commutation relations). Solving the eigenvalue problem eq. 17 yields the well-known relativistic energy relation

eq. 25 \[ E = \pm\,\sqrt{p^2 c^2 + m_e^2 c^4}, \]

with continuous positive-energy solutions describing electrons and negative-energy solutions describing positrons (in the Dirac sea interpretation). No discrete eigenvalues emerge because the free electron can occupy any momentum state. When the electron is at instantaneous rest, there is no momentum and, in a positive-energy domain, one obtains:

\[ E=m_ec^2; \]

that is the famous rest-energy formula.

Hold on. Let us not forget that we were discussing an electron in free fall. Now, we are asserting that it is at rest? What rest means here is indeed an instantaneous rest frame momentarily co-moving with the electron. Locally, over an infinitesimal interval, that co-moving frame sees the electron’s momentum vanish, so the energy equals \(m_e c^2\). Globally, of course, the electron keeps moving relative to any fixed laboratory frame, thus \(p \neq 0\) in the lab frame; but locally, one can always boost to the electron’s instantaneous rest frame. Hence there is no contradiction. One part of the analysis is the standard dispersion relation in flat spacetime with no external fields. The other—electron at rest—is just the limiting case \(p=0\). In fact, one can always make the electron fall freely (or put it under any potential) and then change the frame of reference so that it is at rest. But in a frame where everything is moving at the same speed, the electron’s momentum does not have to be zero.

We now know that in a non-inertial frame where the electron experiences an approximate uniform gravitational field—or equivalently, an observer with acceleration \(g\) in flat spacetime—, the effective potential \(m_e g z\) is added to the Hamiltonian \(\hat{H}_0\):

eq. 26 \[ \hat{H}' = c \alpha\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}} \;+\; m_e c^2 \beta \;+\; m_eg\hat{z}. \]

Though this form is only strictly valid for weak fields or locally uniform acceleration, it illuminates how the energy spectrum can become discrete once appropriate boundary conditions are imposed alike eq. 19. Concretely, if we imagine the electron confined to a region such that \(\psi(z) \to 0\) for \(z\to -\infty\) or for some large negative \(z\), the modified Dirac equation:

eq. 27 \[ \bigl(\hat{H}' - E\bigr)\,\psi(t,\mathbf{x}) = 0 \]

forces the spinor wavefunction to satisfy a relativistic Airy-type differential equation in \(z\). Just as in the non-relativistic case, the interplay between the linear potential and a boundary condition at large negative \(z\) leads to a ladder of quantised energy levels—which depend on the dimensional scale set by \(m_e\), \(g\), and \(\hbar c\), though now more involved because of the spinor structure. Symbolically, one finds discrete values of \(E_n\) (with \(n\) indexing the allowed solutions) rather than a continuum in \(z\), reflecting the fact that the wavefunction must remain normalisable given the linearly growing potential.

Turning to curved spacetime proper—rather than a mere accelerating observer—one must replace \(m_e g z\) by the full gravitational coupling contained in the covariant Dirac equation,

eq. 28 \[ (\mathrm{i}\,\gamma^\mu D_\mu - m_e)\,\psi = 0, \]

having \(D_\mu = \partial_\mu + \Gamma_\mu\) as the covariant derivative acting on the spinor field, and \(\Gamma_\mu = \frac{1}{8} [\gamma^a, \gamma^b] \omega_{\mu ab}\) the spin connection, wherein \(\omega_{\mu ab}\) are the spin coefficients, characterising the coupling between the spinor field and the spacetime curvature.

In a regime of strong curvature, such as near a black hole, there is no global inertial frame, and one cannot globally capture gravity by a single \(m_e g z\) term. Instead, finding energy levels becomes a global problem of solving the Dirac equation with the appropriate metric (e.g. Schwarzschild or Kerr). Locally, free-falling observers still recover \(\hat{H}_0\) in a tiny neighbourhood, by virtue of the equivalence principle, but an observer hovering at a fixed radius \(r\) will see a redshifted spectrum and an effective potential that diverges at the horizon. Far from being a conceptual failure, this manifests the equivalence principle holds locally: an observer in free fall cannot distinguish between being at rest in flat spacetime and falling radially in Schwarzschild geometry, but only in a suitably small neighbourhood where tidal effects are irrelevant.

Consequently, for an electron in a curved background, the energy we attribute to each quantum state depends on the observer’s reference frame (free-falling, hovering, or accelerating). Just as our simpler linear-potential model yields a discretised spectrum for an electron from the viewpoint of an accelerating observer, a similar logic extends to real gravitational fields: inertial frames perceive free propagation (locally continuous energies), while non-inertial or stationary frames in curved spacetime perceive additional structure—either discretisations or redshifts—depending on how boundary conditions are imposed and how severely curved the geometry is. The conclusion is that even in the relativistic domain, the notion of energy spectra is reference-frame dependent, governed by the underlying geometry of spacetime and the local inertial or accelerating coordinates used to describe it.

This is why, in that regime, the Dirac equation must be formulated with the correct tetrads and spin connection to incorporate all the curvature terms, i.e. equation eq. 28. One can still speak of energy levels, but these arise from solving the field equations globally in the curved background, not from a single linear term.

Even so, the logic of our linear-potential analysis remains instructive: the equivalence principle equates uniform acceleration with a homogeneous gravitational field locally, which explains why we can recast \(m g z\) as a coordinate effect for a suitably small patch of spacetime. That same principle guarantees that, in free fall near a black hole, an observer locally recovers the Minkowski vacuum. Yet an observer hovering at fixed \(r\) outside the horizon sees a gravitational redshift and must expend energy to hold position, modifying any energy spectrum they measure. In effect, the difference between these two vantage points parallels the discrepancy we found between inertial and non-inertial frames in simpler settings. The more extreme the spacetime curvature, the more pronounced the difference in how the two observers account for what the electron field (or any quantum field) is doing. Still, one recovers the same local inertial physics at each event, consistent with Einstein’s original equivalence principle.

Gauge Transformations or Shifts in Frames of Reference?

We have seen how energy spectra and wave equations change from one reference frame to another, with gravity and acceleration playing critical roles. An equally crucial aspect is the presence of gauge fields—whether Abelian or not. These gauge fields also respond to changes of frames, but in ways governed by local gauge invariance, which ensures the physical observables remain independent of our specific choice of potentials or local transformations. The question now is whether a gauge transformation of eq. 1 can reintroduce the potential in eq. 2 in another gauge.

In Lagrangian mechanics, a gauge transformation involves modifying the Lagrangian by incorporating the total time derivative of an arbitrary smooth function, \(\chi(\mathbf{x}, t)\), while ensuring that the equations of motion remain unchanged. This transformation is achieved by adding \(\chi(\mathbf{x}, t)\) to the Lagrangian, resulting in a new Lagrangian that satisfies the following condition:

eq. 29 \[ \mathcal{L} \longrightarrow \mathcal{L}' = \mathcal{L} + \frac{\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathrm{d}t}. \]

To examine the gravitational potential, let the gauge function be of the form:

eq. 30 \[ \chi = - m \int \mathrm{d}t \, \mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{x}^\prime, \]

which is reminiscent of the action of a particle of mass \(m\) at rest within a gravitational field. Taking the total time derivative of this gauge function and substituting it and eq. 1 into eq. 29 yields:

eq. 31 \[ \mathcal{L}' = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime\,2} - m \mathbf{g} \cdot \mathbf{x}^\prime. \]

The transformed Lagrangian exhibits the same form as the non-inertial Lagrangian eq. 2, where gravity reappears as an effective potential, despite its absence in the original free-fall frame. It is not a coincidence that through an appropriate gauge transformation a linear gravitational potential can be reintroduced.

From the perspective of non-relativistic quantum mechanics, gauge transformations preserve probabilities, but they significantly alter the energy spectrum and partition functions, resulting in observable consequences in physical systems that respond to these quantities eq. 22. Precedent sections have already provided a suitable exposition of this phenomenon. However, the path integral formulation of quantum mechanics offers another straightforward approach to studying the impact of gauge transformations. Here, it is the propagator \(K\) who encapsulates the transition amplitude between two states, serving as a fundamental element for comprehending both dynamic and thermodynamic properties of the ensemble.

For a free particle of charge \(e\) moving along the time interval \([t_i, t_f]\), the kernel is given by:

\[ K_0(\mathbf{x}_f, t_f; \mathbf{x}_i, t_i) = \left(\frac{m}{2 \pi \mathrm{i} \hbar (t_f - t_i)} \right)^{3/2} \exp \left(\frac{\mathrm{i} m}{2 \hbar (t_f - t_i)} |\mathbf{x}_f - \mathbf{x}_i|^2 \right). \]

Under the gauge transformation defined by eq. 29, the kernel acquires an additional phase factor:

\[ K' = K_0 \exp \left(- \frac{\mathrm{i} e m}{\hbar c} \mathbf{g} \cdot (\mathbf{x}_f - \mathbf{x}_i) + \frac{\mathrm{i} e m}{\hbar c} \int \mathrm{d}t\, \mathbf{g} \cdot \mathbf{x} \right). \]

The modulus of the propagator, which determines the probability amplitude, remains invariant because the phase factor is purely imaginary:

\[ |K'| = |K_0|. \]

Thus, the gauge transformation does not affect the probability of finding the particle at a given point in space and time. It is such an invariance which reflects the fundamental principle of gauge symmetry in electrodynamics: physical observables cannot depend on arbitrary redefinitions of the potentials.

Nonetheless, this does not imply that all physical properties remain unchanged. The phase factor in the kernel corresponds to an effective shift in the particle's energy spectrum. The modified Lagrangian introduces additional terms which represent a momentum shift, a quadratic energy offset, and a linear potential term. And, just as we have seen, these alterations imply the gauge transformation corresponds to introducing a frame of reference where the particle perceives an effective momentum shift and an additional scalar potential. While the transition probabilities remain unaltered, the energy eigenvalues and the structure of energy levels change.

This distinction is crucial. The interpretation of energy levels depends on the choice of gauge, which acts as a selection of a particular coordinate or reference frame in which the physical system is described. The energy spectrum, therefore, encodes information about the gauge freedom, and its changes reflect the system's interaction with the background field encoded in the gauge choice.

Furthermore, in the path integral representation, the partition function can be viewed as an imaginary-time propagator with periodic boundary conditions in the time direction:

eq. 32 \[ Z = \int \mathcal{D}\mathbf{x}(\tau)\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_0^{\hbar\beta}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathcal{L}\bigl[\mathbf{x}(\tau),\dot{\mathbf{x}}(\tau)\bigr]\right). \]

A gauge transformation that adds an imaginary phase to the Euclidean action similarly shifts the effective potential in the exponent. While transition probabilities remain intact due to the invariance of the modulus of the propagator, the energy levels entering \(Z\) and \(F\) do not. This again highlights that thermodynamic quantities like the partition function and free energy encode information about the reference frame or gauge choice, even though the underlying probabilities are invariant.

One can make this more concrete by examining the free particle partition function and then introducing a uniform field. The free particle partition function in three dimensions, confined to a volume \(V\), is given by eq. 9:

\[ Z_0 \propto \left(\frac{m}{2\pi \hbar^2 \beta}\right)^{3/2} V, \]

where we emphasise the dependence on \(\beta\). Since a gravitational-like potential term causes the energy levels to shift by a term linear in \(\mathbf{x}\), the path integral picks up a corresponding factor

eq. 33 \[ \exp\Bigl(-\beta \, m \mathbf{g}\cdot \mathbf{x}\Bigr), \]

modifying \(Z_0\) into \(Z'\). This extra factor depends on the details of the domain of integration and boundary conditions, leading to observable consequences in phenomena such as sedimentation equilibrium in classical contexts or shifts in quantum energy levels.

Despite these gauge-induced differences in partition functions and free energies, the probabilities dictated by the underlying quantum theory remain immune to such redefinitions. The freedom to choose a gauge, or equivalently, to select a particular reference frame, is thus manifest in the thermodynamic sector through changes in the energy spectrum but leaves the absolute transition probabilities between states unaffected. This reconciles the fundamental gauge invariance of quantum dynamics with the gauge dependence of certain thermodynamic or energy-based descriptions.

From the perspective of fibre bundles, a gauge transformation corresponds to a change in the local trivialisation of a principal bundle whose fibres encode the internal degrees of freedom, such as phase factors in the Abelian case or more general group elements for non-Abelian symmetries. Choosing a different gauge is tantamount to selecting a different local section of this bundle, leaving physical observables invariant because they derive from curvature or other gauge-covariant constructs. By contrast, switching to a new frame of reference is a transformation in the tangent bundle or its associated bundles, typically altering how one perceives gravitational or inertial effects at the spacetime level. While one can engineer situations where a gauge transformation brings about a gravity-like potential, this coincidence does not dissolve the deeper geometric differences: gauge bundles handle internal symmetries, whereas reference frames pivot on the structure of spacetime and how one bases vectors in tangent spaces.

The transition to a new reference frame often amounts to redefining coordinate patches and charts in a manifold, re-labelling local trivial patches in a way that changes how one measures distances or times. In comparison, a gauge redefinition can be seen as shifting how one tracks internal charges or group-valued variables, possibly inducing the same functional forms of potentials as those arising from a non-inertial frame choice, but living on a distinct bundle with its own connection. The subtlety is that both methods can yield terms in the Lagrangian resembling gravitational fields, yet they stem from fundamentally different origins in geometry. The effective gravity-like potentials may coincide in specific scenarios but should not be conflated: changing the gauge is not synonymous with changing the reference frame, even if the immediate physical manifestations appear alike.
